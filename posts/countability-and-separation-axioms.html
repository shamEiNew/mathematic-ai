Countability and Separation Axioms

August 24, 2026•Sham topology countability and separation axioms

This note collects four useful observations showing how countability conditions interact with separation axioms. The proofs are elementary, but the examples show why the hypotheses matter.

4.1 A first-countable $T_1$ space has $G_\delta$ singletons

Let $X$ be a first-countable $T_1$ space. Fix $x\in X$, and let $\{B_n(x):n\in\mathbb N\}$ be a countable local base at $x$. We claim that

$$\{x\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n(x).$$

The inclusion from left to right is immediate. Conversely, suppose $y\ne x$. Since $X$ is $T_1$, the set $X\setminus\{y\}$ is open and contains $x$. The local-base property gives some $n$ with $x\in B_n(x)\subseteq X\setminus\{y\}$. Hence $y\notin B_n(x)$, so $y$ is not in the intersection. Thus every singleton is a $G_\delta$-set.

4.2 A countable base need not make a space $T_1$

Consider $X=\mathbb N\cup\{\infty\}$, where the points of $\mathbb N$ are isolated and the neighborhoods of $\infty$ are the sets

$$U_N=\{\infty\}\cup\{n\in\mathbb N:n\ge N\},\qquad N\in\mathbb N.$$

The family consisting of all singletons $\{n\}$ for $n\in\mathbb N$, together with the sets $U_N$, is a countable basis. However, the space is not $T_1$: every neighborhood of $\infty$ contains all sufficiently large natural numbers, so no neighborhood of $\infty$ can avoid a prescribed tail point. This illustrates that second countability alone does not imply the separation axioms.

4.3 An uncountable set has uncountably many limit points in a second-countable space

Let $X$ have a countable basis $\mathcal B=\{B_1,B_2,\ldots\}$, and let $A\subseteq X$ be uncountable. We show that the derived set $A'$ is uncountable.

Assume, toward a contradiction, that $A'$ is countable. Choose $x\in A\setminus A'$. Since $x$ is not a limit point of $A$, there is an open neighborhood $U_x$ such that $U_x\cap(A\setminus\{x\})=\varnothing$. Choose a basis element $B_{n(x)}$ with $x\in B_{n(x)}\subseteq U_x$. For each fixed basis element $B_n$, there can be at most one point $x\in A\setminus A'$ with $B_{n(x)}=B_n$, because $B_n$ contains no other point of $A$. Therefore $A\setminus A'$ is countable, being indexed by the countable basis. Since $A'$ was assumed countable, $A=(A\setminus A')\cup A'$ would be countable, a contradiction. Thus $A'$ is uncountable.

4.4 Every compact metric space has a countable basis

Let $(X,d)$ be compact. For every $n\in\mathbb N$, the family of balls $\{B(x,1/n):x\in X\}$ covers $X$. By compactness, choose a finite subcover and call its set of centers $F_n$. Define

$$\mathcal B=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{B(a,1/n):a\in F_n\}.$$

Each $F_n$ is finite, so $\mathcal B$ is a countable union of finite sets and is therefore countable. It remains to check that it is a basis. If $U$ is open and $x\in U$, choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Choose $n$ so large that $2/n<\varepsilon$. Since $F_n$ covers $X$, there is $a\in F_n$ with $d(x,a)<1/n$. Then $x\in B(a,1/n)$, and for every $y\in B(a,1/n)$,

$$d(y,x)\le d(y,a)+d(a,x)<\frac{2}{n}<\varepsilon.$$

Hence $B(a,1/n)\subseteq B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Thus $\mathcal B$ is a countable basis, and every compact metric space is second-countable.

These results form a useful chain: countable local information controls singleton descriptions, while compactness supplies the finite selections needed to build a global countable basis.
