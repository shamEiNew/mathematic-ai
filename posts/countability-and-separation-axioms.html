Countability and Separation Axioms

August 24, 2026•Sham topology countability and separation axioms munkres

This note collects observations showing how countability conditions interact with separation axioms. The proofs are elementary, but the examples show why the hypotheses matter.

4.1 A first-countable $T_1$ space has $G_\delta$ singletons

Let $X$ be a first-countable $T_1$ space. Fix $x\in X$, and let $\{B_n(x):n\in\mathbb N\}$ be a countable local base at $x$. We claim that

$$\{x\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n(x).$$

The inclusion from left to right is immediate. Conversely, suppose $y\ne x$. Since $X$ is $T_1$, the set $X\setminus\{y\}$ is open and contains $x$. The local-base property gives some $n$ with $x\in B_n(x)\subseteq X\setminus\{y\}$. Hence $y\notin B_n(x)$, so $y$ is not in the intersection. Thus every singleton is a $G_\delta$-set.

4.2 The Arens–Fort space is not first-countable

Let $X=\mathbb N\times\mathbb N\cup\{p\}$, and define the topology on $X$ as follows:

  1. All subsets of $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ that do not contain $p$ are open.
  2. A subset containing $p$ is open if, from each row $\{(m,n):n\in\mathbb N\}$, only finitely many points are excluded.

Note that this is a valid topology: $\varnothing$ and $X$ are included, since $X$ excludes no points. Moreover, arbitrary unions of sets of the second type are open, since if $p$ belongs to the union, then in each row the union still omits only finitely many points. Finite intersections are also open because the union of two finite subsets of a row is finite.

Since each point is isolated and open, each singleton is a $G_\delta$-set. Also,

$$\{p\}=\bigcap_{k\geq 1}\left(\{p\}\cup\{(k,n):n\in\mathbb N\}\right).$$

Suppose we have a countable basis $\{B_1,B_2,\ldots\}$ at $p$. Now construct an open set as follows. Choose $x_k=(m_k,n_k)$, where $m_k,n_k\in\mathbb N$, such that $x_k\in B_k$. Such a choice is possible because we exclude only finitely many elements from each row, so we can select an element from the remaining points. Call this point $x_k$.

Since each singleton is open, this implies that $X\setminus\{x_1,x_2,\ldots\}$ is open and contains $p$. But $x_k$ is not in this open set, even though each basis element $B_k$ contains $x_k$. Therefore, $B_k$ cannot be contained in $X\setminus\{x_1,x_2,\ldots\}$ for any $k$. This construction is possible for every countable basis at $p$, and hence the Arens–Fort space cannot have a countable basis at $p$.

4.3 An uncountable set has uncountably many limit points in a second-countable space

Let $X$ have a countable basis $\mathcal B=\{B_1,B_2,\ldots\}$, and let $A\subseteq X$ be uncountable. We show that the derived set $A'$ is uncountable.

Assume, toward a contradiction, that $A'$ is countable. Choose $x\in A\setminus A'$. Since $x$ is not a limit point of $A$, there is an open neighborhood $U_x$ such that $U_x\cap(A\setminus\{x\})=\varnothing$. Choose a basis element $B_{n(x)}$ with $x\in B_{n(x)}\subseteq U_x$. For each fixed basis element $B_n$, there can be at most one point $x\in A\setminus A'$ with $B_{n(x)}=B_n$, because $B_n$ contains no other point of $A$. Therefore $A\setminus A'$ is countable, being indexed by the countable basis. Since $A'$ was assumed countable, $A=(A\setminus A')\cup A'$ would be countable, a contradiction. Thus $A'$ is uncountable.

4.4 Every compact metric space has a countable basis

Let $(X,d)$ be compact. For every $n\in\mathbb N$, the family of balls $\{B(x,1/n):x\in X\}$ covers $X$. By compactness, choose a finite subcover and call its set of centers $F_n$. Define

$$\mathcal B=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{B(a,1/n):a\in F_n\}.$$

Each $F_n$ is finite, so $\mathcal B$ is a countable union of finite sets and is therefore countable. It remains to check that it is a basis. If $U$ is open and $x\in U$, choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Choose $n$ so large that $2/n<\varepsilon$. Since $F_n$ covers $X$, there is $a\in F_n$ with $d(x,a)<1/n$. Then $x\in B(a,1/n)$, and for every $y\in B(a,1/n)$,

$$d(y,x)\le d(y,a)+d(a,x)<\frac{2}{n}<\varepsilon.$$

Hence $B(a,1/n)\subseteq B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Thus $\mathcal B$ is a countable basis, and every compact metric space is second-countable.

These results form a useful chain: countable local information controls singleton descriptions, while compactness supplies the finite selections needed to build a global countable basis.

(4.5)(a) Every metrizable space with countable dense subset has a countable basis.

Let $X$ be a metrizable space with a countable dense subset.

\[ A \subseteq X, \qquad \overline{A}=X. \]

Let

\[ A=\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n,\ldots\}. \]
\[ \mathcal{B} = \left\{ B\left(x_i,\frac{1}{n}\right) : i,n\in\mathbb{N} \right\}. \]

Let $U\subseteq X$ be open.

By density of $\overline{A}$,

\[ U\cap A\neq\varnothing. \]

Let $x\in U$. As $U$ is open,

\[ \exists\,\varepsilon>0 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U. \]

Now $B(x,\varepsilon)$ is nbd of $x$.

Choose $N\in\mathbb{N}$ s.t.

\[ \frac{2}{N}<\varepsilon. \]

Choose

\[ x_i\in B\left(x,\frac{1}{N}\right)\cap A. \]

Let

\[ y\in B\left(x_i,\frac{1}{N}\right). \]

Then

\[ d(x,y) \leq d(x,x_i)+d(x_i,y) < \frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{N} = \frac{2}{N} < \varepsilon. \]

Hence

\[ y\in B(x,\varepsilon). \]

Therefore

\[ B\left(x_i,\frac{1}{N}\right) \subseteq B(x,\varepsilon) \subseteq U. \]

Satisfying the condition of basis.

Therefore

\[ \mathcal{B} = \left\{ B\left(x_i,\frac{1}{n}\right) : i,n\in\mathbb{N} \right\} \]

is a countable basis.

(b) Every metrizable Lindelöf space has a countable basis.

Let $X$ be a metrizable Lindelöf space.

\[ X\neq\varnothing. \]

Consider the collection, for $x\in X$,

\[ \bigcup_n \mathcal{B}_n = \left\{ B\left(x,\frac{1}{n}\right):x\in X \right\}. \]
\[ X \subseteq \bigcup_{x\in X}B\left(x,\frac{1}{n}\right). \]

$\therefore$ $X$ is covered by this collection.

As $X$ is Lindelöf we have a countable collection

\[ B_{x_1},B_{x_2},\ldots,B_{x_k},\ldots \]
\[ \mathcal{B}_n = \left\{ B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right):k\in\mathbb{N} \right\}. \]
\[ \mathcal{B} = \left\{ B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right): n,k\in\mathbb{N} \right\}. \]

Let $U\subseteq X$ be an open subset and $x\in U$.

There exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that

\[ B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U. \]

Choose $n\in\mathbb{N}$ such that

\[ \frac{2}{n}<\varepsilon. \]

Since

\[ \left\{ B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right):k\in\mathbb{N} \right\} \]

covers $X$, there is some $k$ such that

\[ x\in B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right). \]

Let

\[ y\in B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right). \]

Then

\[ d(x,y) \leq d(x,x_{nk})+d(x_{nk},y) < \frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n} = \frac{2}{n} < \varepsilon. \]

Thus

\[ y\in B(x,\varepsilon). \]

Therefore

\[ x\in B\left(x_{nk},\frac{1}{n}\right) \subseteq B(x,\varepsilon) \subseteq U. \]

Hence $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies the condition of basis.

(4.6)(a) $\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$ is not metrizable

$\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$ is the lower limit topology with the basis

\[ \mathcal{B} = \{[a,b):a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}. \]

We show that $\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$ is not second countable.

For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$,

\[ [x,x+1) \]

is open in $\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$.

Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis,

\[ \exists\,B_x\in\mathcal{B} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad x\in B_x\subseteq[x,x+1). \]

If $x<y$ and $B_x=B_y$, then

\[ x\in B_y\subseteq[y,y+1), \]

which is impossible since $x<y$.

Thus

\[ x\neq y\implies B_x\neq B_y. \]

Hence

\[ x\longmapsto B_x \]

is an injection

\[ \mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}. \]

But $\mathcal{B}$ is countable, a contradiction.

Therefore

\[ \mathbb{R}_{\ell} \text{ is not second countable.} \]

Note that if a space is second countable, it can have at most countably many pairwise disjoint open subsets.

(b) Finally, we prove Lindelöfnes of $\mathbb{R}_l$ Let $\{U_\alpha\}$ be an open cover for $\mathbb{R}_l$.

Now we can construct a refinement such that for each $x$,

\[ \exists\,[a_x,b_x)\subseteq U_\alpha. \]

We consider the collection

\[ \{[a_j,b_j)\}_{j\in J}. \]

It is enough to prove that

\[ \{[a_j,b_j)\}_{j\in J} \]

admits a countable subcover.

Let

\[ C=\bigcup_{j\in J}(a_j,b_j). \]

Claim:

\[ \mathbb{R}-C \text{ is countable}. \]

Suppose

\[ x\in\mathbb{R}-C. \]

Then

\[ x\notin(a_j,b_j) \qquad\forall j\in J. \]

Hence $x$ does not lie in between $a_j$ and $b_j$.

Since the collection covers $\mathbb{R}$,

\[ x\in[a_\beta,b_\beta) \]

for some $\beta$.

As

\[ x\notin(a_\beta,b_\beta), \]

we must have

\[ x=a_\beta. \]

For any two points

\[ x,y\in\mathbb{R}-C, \]

choose corresponding intervals

\[ x=a_\beta, \qquad y=a_\gamma. \]

If $x\neq y$, then they belong to different intervals in the corresponding construction.

Choose

\[ q_x\in\mathbb{Q} \]

such that

\[ x<q_x<b_\beta. \]

If $x<y$, then

\[ q_x<y \]

can be chosen whenever the corresponding intervals are disjoint in the required construction.

Thus

\[ x\neq y\implies q_x\neq q_y. \]

We have an injection

\[ x\longmapsto q_x \]

from $\mathbb{R}-C$ into $\mathbb{Q}$.

Therefore

\[ \mathbb{R}-C \]

is countable.

Now for each element in $\mathbb{R}-C$, choose an element from

\[ \{(a_j,b_j)\}_{j\in J}. \]

Since $\mathbb{R}-C$ is countable, the subcollection is countable.

Further, $C$ has a countable subcover from $\mathbb{R}$ (usual topology).

Therefore,

\[ \mathbb{R}_{\ell} \]

is a union of countable subcovers. Therefore, $\mathbb{R}_l$ is lindelof and not second countabl which implies its not metrizable because lindelof metrizable space is second countable.

(c) A second countable space has countably many pairwise disjoint open subsets.

Let

\[ \mathcal{B}=\{B_1,B_2,\ldots\} \]

be the basis of our space $X$.

Let

\[ \mathcal{A}=\{A_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I} \]

be a collection of pairwise disjoint subsets, where $A_\alpha$ is open.

Since $A_\alpha$ is open,

\[ \exists\,B_k \quad\text{s.t.}\quad B_k\subseteq A_\alpha. \]

If

\[ A_\alpha\cap A_\beta=\varnothing \qquad \text{for }\alpha\neq\beta, \]

then

\[ B_k\not\subseteq A_\beta \qquad \text{for }\alpha\neq\beta. \]

Each $A_\alpha$ has a corresponding basis element contained in $A_\alpha$,

\[ B_{k_\alpha}\subseteq A_\alpha. \]

Therefore

\[ A_\alpha\longmapsto B_{k_\alpha} \]

is an injection.

Since $\mathcal{B}$ is countable,

\[ \mathcal{A}\text{ is countable}. \]

(d) $I_0^2$ is not metrizable.

$I_0^2$ is the ordered square topology.

Define order

\[ (a,b)<(c,d) \iff (a<c) \text{ or } (a=c\text{ and }b<d). \]

Now

\[ \{x\}\times(0,1) \]

is an open set.

The collection

\[ \left\{ \{x\}\times(0,1): x\in[0,1] \right\} \]

is uncountable.

Moreover, if $x\neq y$,

\[ \bigl(\{x\}\times(0,1)\bigr) \cap \bigl(\{y\}\times(0,1)\bigr) = \varnothing. \]

$\therefore$ it has an uncountable collection of pairwise disjoint open subsets.

Therefore $I_0^2$ is not second countable.

Since $I_0^2$ is Lindelöf, if it were metrizable, then by part (b) it would be second countable, a contradiction.

Hence

\[ I_0^2\text{ is not metrizable}. \]
